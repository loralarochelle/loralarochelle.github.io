Welcome! I am a first-year PhD student in Biostatistics at Brown University. My research interests include Bayesian statistics, causal inference, and missing data methods, with applications to public health, health policy, and infectious disease epidemiology.

Prior to Brown, I studied mathematics and statistics at Colby College and completed research experiences at the Gulf of Maine Research Institute and the Yale School of Public Health.

Current Interests

I am broadly interested in Bayesian statistics, causal inference, and missing data methods. My research interests center on the development of statistical methodology for drawing reliable inferences from complex and incomplete data, particularly in settings where standard assumptions may be difficult to justify or verify.

A major focus of my current interests is the study of missing and imperfectly observed data. I am particularly interested in principled approaches to handling structural missingness, improving uncertainty quantification, and reducing bias in analyses involving underrepresented populations. More broadly, I am interested in methods that allow researchers to assess the sensitivity of their conclusions to assumptions that cannot be directly tested from the observed data.

I am also interested in nonparametric Bayesian methods, machine learning approaches for understanding treatment effect heterogeneity, and statistical methods for causal inference. My applied interests include public health and health policy primarily, but I am also interested in applications to infectious disease epidemiology and environmental systems.

Past Experience

Gulf of Maine Research Institute (2024)

In the summer of 2024, I worked as a research fellow in the Integrated Systems Ecology Lab at the Gulf of Maine Research Institute in Portland, Maine as part of the REU program. Under the mentorship of Dr. Katherine Mills and Dr. Bart DiFiore, I worked on a project at the intersection of statistics and ecology focused on measuring and modeling changes in fish condition in the Northeast.

My research examined relationships between fish length and weight, evaluated temporal changes in the proportion of large fish, and developed a population model to assess how changes in fish condition influence spawning stock abundance.

By identifying species for which condition parameters have significantly changed, this work provided updated estimates that can improve reporting accuracy and support fisheries management efforts.

Technical Report

Yale School of Public Health (2025)

The following summer, I worked as a research fellow at the Yale School of Public Health in the Epidemiology of Microbial Diseases department under the supervision of Dr. Stephanie Perniciaro and Shelby Golden, M.S.

Our project investigated the potential implementation of a higher-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) in Nepal by modeling pneumococcal trends following the introduction of the 13-valent PCV in 2015.

To evaluate vaccine impact and serotype dynamics, we employed time series modeling with counterfactual scenarios, association rule mining, and Bayesian hierarchical modeling.

Our analyses identified a notable increase in carriage of non-PCV-targeted serotypes, consistent with potential serotype replacement following vaccine introduction.

Applying to Graduate School

As someone who recently went through the graduate admissions process and benefited greatly from online forums and similar posts from other students, I wanted to pay it forward by documenting my experience applying to graduate school in biostatistics. This page summarizes my application cycle, interview experiences, and the factors that ultimately influenced my decision.

Applying directly from undergraduate study during a challenging funding climate, I was not especially confident in my chances of being admitted to a PhD program on my first attempt. I was extremely pleased with how the cycle turned out and am thrilled to be pursuing my PhD in Biostatistics at Brown University. Brown was actually my top choice when I began applying. Many people advised me not to have a favorite school because admissions can be unpredictable, but I think it is perfectly fine to get excited about a program—it might just work out.

How I Chose Where to Apply

  1. Research Fit. If you are considering a PhD, this should be the single most important factor in deciding where to apply. I read faculty profiles, explored department websites, and skimmed recent papers from researchers whose interests seemed most aligned with mine. There were a few programs that I liked on paper, but where I had difficulty finding research that truly excited me. Even after receiving interviews from some of these programs, I found it harder to remain enthusiastic about them compared to schools where I could easily envision myself working with multiple faculty members. In my experience, research fit matters more than rankings, prestige, or almost any other factor. Prioritize faculty whose work genuinely interests you.
  2. Location and Cost of Living. Graduate stipends go much further in some places than others, so I paid close attention to whether I could realistically live comfortably on the funding package offered by each program. While many applicants focus primarily on stipend amounts, I found it more useful to consider the relationship between funding and local housing costs, transportation, and other living expenses. I did not have a strong preference for a rural, suburban, or urban environment when I was applying, which is probably evident from the variety of schools on my list. I did rule out some programs located in very high-cost cities (NYC and LA specifically), even when I found the academic programs appealing. I also limited the number of schools in the South that I applied to, simply because I did not think I would thrive in a significantly warmer climate. While research fit remained my top priority, I believed that being comfortable in the place where I would spend the next five or more years was an important consideration as well.
  3. Advice from Mentors. My faculty mentors helped me assess which programs would be the strongest fit and how different departments were viewed within the field.
  4. Curriculum. Required coursework and elective offerings reveal a lot about a department's priorities and strengths. Looking closely at a program's curriculum helped me understand what areas of research were most prominent, what skills faculty considered most important for their students to develop, and how much flexibility students had to pursue their own interests. Strong mathematical training was particularly important to me given my background in pure mathematics, so I prioritized programs that allowed students to take courses across departments, particularly in mathematics, statistics, and computer science. Some programs emphasized theoretical foundations more heavily, while others focused on applications or interdisciplinary training. Thinking carefully about the type of training I wanted to receive helped me identify programs that were the best fit for my long-term goals.
  5. Alumni Outcomes. I looked closely at where graduates ended up after completing their degrees and whether alumni with research interests similar to mine were pursuing careers that appealed to me. Some programs had extensive information available, including dedicated alumni pages with recent placements and career updates, while others provided little information and I had to do a bit more digging. Examining alumni outcomes gave me a better sense of the opportunities available after graduation and the types of careers a program was best positioned to support. I paid attention to recent graduates in particular, as their experiences were likely the most reflective of the current state of the program and job market.

Looking back, I probably applied to more programs than I needed to. My original list consisted entirely of schools that I knew I would have been thrilled to attend, and if I could do it again, I might have focused my efforts on that smaller set of programs. Fortunately, every school that ultimately admitted me was somewhere I would have been excited to attend, which made for a very hard decision as the cycle came to a close.

Application Advice

  • Start earlier than you think you need to.
  • Have trusted mentors review your materials.
  • Talk to students who have recently gone through the process.
  • Do not obsess over admissions statistics posted online.
  • Research each program thoroughly before applying.

I believe the strengths of my application were my quantitative background in mathematics, statistics, and computer science; strong letters of recommendation; the extensive research I conducted on the programs to which I applied; and a compelling personal story as a first-generation college student from rural Maine. Some of my perceived weaknesses were that I did not have extensive methodological research experience and that I was applying directly from undergrad. At the time, I was worried that not having a master's degree would put me at a disadvantage. I found that many of the applicants I met during interviews were also applying straight from undergrad, and several programs explicitly stated that they had no preference between applicants coming directly from undergraduate institutions and those holding master's degrees. If you are considering applying directly from undergrad, don't let that fact alone discourage you from applying.

Interviewing

Visit days and interviews can be exhausting. You will spend hours meeting faculty, students, and administrators, often while traveling and operating on limited sleep. It is okay not to be at your absolute best every moment, but staying engaged and curious goes a long way. There will be things that are out of your control (delayed flights, sickness, etc.) and that is okay.

My Advice

  • Wear comfortable shoes. If you do not have comfortable shoes that are appropriate, buy some.
  • Learn about faculty research, but do not try to read every paper. Come up with 1-3 thoughtful questions about their work.
  • Prepare FAR more questions than you think you will need.
  • Know your own research inside and out.

Questions I Frequently Asked

  1. How is the program evolving to reflect changes in the field?
  2. How are TA and RA positions assigned?
  3. How do advisors support the transition to independent research?
  4. What makes this program unique?
  5. Where do graduates typically end up after finishing (academia, industry, government)?
  6. What collaborations exist across departments?

When speaking with current students, I was especially interested in how supported they felt by faculty, how happy they were living in the area, and what ultimately convinced them to choose that program over others.

Choosing a Program

Once admissions decisions arrive, the process becomes both easier and more difficult. If you applied only to programs that genuinely interested you, there may be several excellent options to choose from. Below are the main things I considered when weighing my options.

  1. Student and Faculty Interactions. While I tried not to let a single day completely shape my perception of a program, these visits provided great insight into how supported students felt, how collaborative the environment was, and how faculty interacted with their advisees. I paid close attention to whether students seemed genuinely enthusiastic about their work, whether they spoke positively about their advisors and peers, and whether there appeared to be a strong sense of community within the department. Although visit days only provide a snapshot of a program, they can reveal aspects of the graduate student experience that are difficult to learn from any online resources.
  2. Funding. Full funding, stipend levels, and opportunities for research assistantships were all important considerations. Also researching the cost of living in the area (and consulting with current students about this) was helpful in understanding how far that stipend would go.
  3. Research Fit (Again). Ultimately, the availability of research that genuinely excited me outweighed nearly every other factor.

Final Thoughts

Graduate admissions can feel overwhelming, unpredictable, and highly personal. Focus on identifying programs that genuinely fit your interests, prepare the strongest application you can, and remember that admissions outcomes do not define your potential as a researcher.

If you have questions about applying to graduate school in biostatistics, statistics, or a related quantitative field, feel free to reach out. I am always happy to chat with prospective applicants!
